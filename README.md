# Why are we here?

*A reflection on learning to program in a year when a machine will do it on request.*

In 2026 I can describe a generative poster in one sentence and get something
running back. The tools that do this improve roughly every six months. So the
honest version of "why are we here" is not *what is programming good for* — it
is why **I**, as a designer, should spend a semester on a skill that is being
automated in front of me. I do not think the answer is obvious, and I distrust
the comfortable one: that we learn the fundamentals so we can "use the tools
better". That sentence has been said about every tool that ever replaced a craft,
and it is usually a way of not saying what actually happens to the craft.

## Describing is not specifying

The first thing this course made concrete for me is the gap between *describing*
and *specifying*. Asking for "a poster that feels unsettling" is easy. Getting
something I actually want out of that request is a different skill, and it is not
one the request itself teaches. When I write a rule like
`damage = chaos * (row / ROWS) ** 2`, I am not typing instructions at a machine —
I am deciding, precisely, how disorder should accumulate down a page. That
decision is mine. The machine can execute it; it cannot know that the top of the
grid should stay calm while the bottom comes apart. Producing a picture is cheap
now. *Deciding what it should be* is not, and that is the part I am here to
practise.

## Someone still has to read

If a machine writes the code, someone still has to judge whether it is any good.
Week two is built entirely around that: four programs that run without an error
message and are wrong, and three candidate implementations where exactly one
meets the brief. Reading a program to find the fault is a different act from
writing one from nothing, and it is the act I expect to do most often.
Understanding the system is what lets me say *why* a plausible result is wrong,
rather than only that I do not like it — and a designer who cannot say why is not
in a position to sign anything off.

## The medium is not neutral

Ihde's argument from the lecture stays with me: technologies are never neutral
tools, they shape what I perceive as possible. A painter understands paint; a
photographer understands light. If I direct a system whose behaviour I do not
understand, I lose the ability to see the parts of the work that the system
decided for me. The loss is invisible, which is exactly what makes it expensive.
Learning the medium is how I keep the authorship.

## Where I land

I do not think the discipline survives by defending the typing. It survives
because specifying, reading, and judging are design acts, and design was never
only about execution. The machine is a faster hand and a tireless drafter; it is
not the person who decides what is worth making or whether the thing in front of
them is right. That person is the one I want to be, and that is why I am here.
